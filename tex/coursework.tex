\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This project addresses the problem of domain shift in computer vision, focusing on the transition from high-quality
synthetic and studio-grade image data to real-world user-generated photographs with variable quality. A unified
training pipeline is developed to investigate domain adaptation strategies that enable effective transfer between
source and target domains. Baseline image classification models (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16) are implemented and evaluated
for robustness under varying real-world image quality.

A comparative analysis examines performance degradation across domains and assesses the effectiveness of different
adaptation approaches. The project deliverables include a reproducible training pipeline, a public repository of
experimental results, and a course work describing the methodology and findings. The outcomes contribute to a deeper
understanding of domain adaptation challenges and provide practical insights for developing robust computer vision
systems.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Problem Relevance}
Computer vision models often fail when deployed in real-world conditions due to the domain gap between controlled
training data (synthetic/studio) and unpredictable user-generated content. This project systematically analyses the
synthetic-to-real shift, focusing on robustness against common image quality degradations.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
The research investigates the robustness of modern computer vision models under synthetic-to-real domain shift,
focusing on how real-world image variability affect classification performance. The goal is to quantify performance
degradation across domains and explore reliable ways to measure cross-domain generalization.

\subsection{Project Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Develop a structured experimental pipeline for measuring and visualising domain shift from high-quality
	      synthetic/studio images to real-world photographs, using the VisDA-2017 benchmark as a representative testbed.
	\item Train baseline classification models (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16) on the source domain and quantitatively assess the
	      cross-domain performance gap without any adaptation, applying standard data augmentation throughout.
	\item Implement and compare at least two distinct domain adaptation strategies, covering both discrepancy-based alignment
	      (MMD, CORAL) and adversarial feature learning (DANN), and integrate them into both convolutional and transformer
	      backbones.
	\item Carry out a comprehensive comparative analysis across all models and conditions (source-only, adapted, and augmented
	      inputs), examining accuracy degradation, feature distribution shifts via t-SNE/PCA, and relative robustness of CNN and
	      ViT architectures.
	\item Publish a fully reproducible GitHub repository containing the entire training/evaluation pipeline, pretrained
	      checkpoints, and a detailed final report that documents the methodology, experimental findings, and practical
	      recommendations for synthetic-to-real transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

Domain shift remains a fundamental challenge for visual recognition models deployed outside the laboratory. Early work
on domain adaptation introduced methods that align feature distributions across domains. Discrepancy-based approaches
minimise statistical distances such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy \cite{gretton2012kernel, long2015learning} or align
second-order statistics via CORAL \cite{sun2016deep}. Adversarial strategies, pioneered by Domain-Adversarial Neural
Networks (DANN) \cite{ganin2016domain} and later extended by ADDA \cite{tzeng2017adversarial}, employ a gradient
reversal layer to learn domain-invariant representations. These techniques provide principled ways to reduce the gap
between source and target distributions without requiring target labels.

The synthetic-to-real gap is a particularly severe instance of domain shift. High-quality synthetic or studio images
differ substantially from real-world photographs in texture, lighting, and background clutter. Richter \textit{et~al.}
\cite{richter2016playing} demonstrated that large-scale synthetic data can be generated from game engines, while
Shrivastava \textit{et~al.} \cite{shrivastava2017learning} refined synthetic images using adversarial training to
improve realism and downstream task performance. Despite such advances, models trained on pristine data often fail when
confronted with the unpredictable degradations typical of user-generated content.

Alongside algorithmic progress, standardised benchmarks have become essential for measuring robustness. ImageNet-C
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} systematically evaluates model resilience to common corruptions such as noise, blur,
and weather artifacts, but it does not explicitly model the structured domain gap between synthetic and real imagery.
The VisDA-2017 challenge \cite{visda2017} directly targets synthetic-to-real adaptation with a large-scale 12-class
dataset, providing a rigorous testbed for domain adaptation methods.

More recently, Vision Transformers (ViTs) \cite{dosovitskiy2020image} have emerged as strong alternatives to
convolutional networks. While their performance on clean benchmarks often surpasses that of CNNs, their behaviour under
domain shift and image quality degradation remains an active area of investigation. Understanding the relative
robustness of convolutional and transformer-based architectures is therefore crucial for designing reliable real-world
systems.

In summary, a mature body of domain adaptation techniques exists, yet a systematic comparison of their effectiveness
for synthetic-to-real transfer—especially when combined with modern architectures and realistic visual
corruptions—remains incomplete. This project addresses that gap by evaluating both discrepancy-based and adversarial
adaptation strategies on a challenging synthetic-to-real benchmark, using ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 as representative
backbones.

\begin{itemize}

\section{Methods Research \& Dataset Search}
\subsection{Methods of Domain Adaptation}
Several domain adaptation approaches have been proposed in computer vision, with Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, CORAL \cite{sun2016deep} and DANN \cite{long2015learning} among the most widely used in
classification tasks. This work focuses on the study and application of these strategies.

\subsubsection{MMD}
Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is a non-parametric distance metric used to measure the distribution difference between
source and target domains in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The main idea is to minimize the discrepancy
between feature representations of two domains during training.

Given source domain samples $X_s=\{x_i^s\}_{i=1}^{n_s}$ and target domain samples $X_t=\{x_j^t\}_{j=1}^{n_t}$, the
squared MMD distance is defined as

\[
	\text{MMD}^2(X_s,X_t)=\left\|\frac{1}{n_s}\sum_{i=1}^{n_s}\phi(x_i^s)-\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{j=1}^{n_t}\phi(x_j^t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2
\]

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is a feature mapping function into RKHS $\mathcal{H}$. In practice, kernel functions such as
Gaussian RBF kernel are used to compute MMD efficiently.

\subsubsection{CORAL}
Correlation Alignment (CORAL) is a domain adaptation method that aligns the second-order statistics of source and
target feature distributions by minimizing the difference between their covariance matrices.

Let $C_s$ and $C_t$ denote the covariance matrices of source and target features respectively. The CORAL loss is
defined as

\[
	\mathcal{L}_{CORAL}=\frac{1}{4d^2}\|C_s-C_t\|_F^2
\]

where $d$ is the feature dimension and $\|\cdot\|_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm. By matching covariance statistics,
CORAL reduces domain shift while keeping feature representations discriminative.

\subsubsection{DANN}
Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) is an adversarial learning framework that aims to learn domain-invariant
feature representations using a gradient reversal layer.

The objective function consists of two parts: classification loss and domain discrimination loss:

\[
	\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_y(\hat{y},y)-\lambda \mathcal{L}_d(\hat{d},d)
\]

where $\mathcal{L}_y$ is the task-specific loss (e.g., cross-entropy loss), $\mathcal{L}_d$ is the domain
classification loss, and $\lambda$ is a trade-off hyperparameter. The gradient reversal layer multiplies the gradient
by $-1$ during backpropagation to enforce domain confusion and promote domain-invariant features.

\subsubsection{Summary}
In summary, MMD, CORAL, and DANN address domain shift from complementary perspectives: distribution matching in RKHS,
second-order statistical alignment, and adversarial domain confusion. Together, they provide representative strategies
for learning domain-invariant features, forming a solid foundation for the comparative analysis conducted in this work.

\subsection{Suitable Dataset}
VisDA-2017 \cite{visda2017} is the largest synth-to-real dataset, introduced in Visual Domain Adaptation Challenge. It
contains 12 classes, with 152K training images and 55K validation images. The dataset provides a challenging
large-scale setting for unsupervised domain adaptation, making it highly suitable for research.

\subsection{Key Challenges}
The primary challenge in synthetic-to-real domain adaptation is the significant domain gap: models trained on clean
data struggle with the variability of real-world images, including differences in lighting, texture, and background.
Another issue is over-adaptation, where strong alignment of feature distributions to enforce domain invariance may
suppress task-relevant information and reduce overall performance.

\subsection{ResNet50 Pre-training}
In the first stage, a ResNet50 model was pre-trained for image classification on the source (synthetic) domain. The
network was initialized with random weights and optimized using the Adam optimizer to minimize cross-entropy loss.
Standard data augmentation techniques (random cropping and horizontal flipping) were applied during training.

The pre-training process lasted 16 epochs. During the first five epochs, 20\% of the source training data was used as a
validation subset. Starting from epoch 6, the validation approach was modified: the validation set was replaced with
data sampled from the target (real-world) domain. This change was introduced to obtain an early estimate of
cross-domain generalization.

Figure~\ref{fig:resnet50_pretraining} presents the training and validation loss (left) and Top-1/Top-5 accuracy
(right).

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{resnet50_pretraining.png}
	\caption{\textit{Training and validation loss (left) and accuracy (right) during ResNet50 pre-training.}}
	\label{fig:resnet50_pretraining}
\end{figure}

\newpage

During the first five epochs, training and validation metrics show similar trends, indicating consistent performance
within the source domain. Training loss decreases steadily from approximately 1.5 to below 0.6, while Top-1 accuracy
improves from 48\% to around 80\%. Validation performance on the source-domain split follows a comparable trajectory.

After switching the validation set to the target domain (epoch 6 onward), a significant performance drop is observed.
Validation loss increases sharply (exceeding 6.0), and Top-1 accuracy decreases to approximately 20–25\%, while
training performance continues to improve. By the final epoch, training Top-1 accuracy approaches 90\%, whereas
target-domain validation accuracy remains near 23\%.

This behavior clearly demonstrates a crucial domain gap between synthetic and real data. While the model effectively
fits the source distribution, it generalizes poorly to the target domain. These results justify the need for explicit
domain adaptation strategies in subsequent stages of the project.

\subsection{ViT-B/16 Pre-training}
In the first stage, a ViT-B/16 model was pre-trained for image classification on the source (synthetic) domain. The
network was initialized with random weights and optimized using the Adam optimizer to minimize cross-entropy loss.
Standard data augmentation techniques were applied during training.

Figure~\ref{fig:vitb16_pretraining} presents the training and validation loss (left) and Top-1/Top-5 accuracy (right).

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{vitb16_pretraining.png}
	\caption{\textit{Training and validation loss (left) and accuracy (right) during ViT-B/16 pre-training.}}
	\label{fig:vitb16_pretraining}
\end{figure}

During the 16 epochs of pre-training, a stark divergence between training and validation metrics is evident from the
very first epoch. Training loss decreases consistently from roughly 1.5 to under 0.4, while validation loss steadily
increases from approximately 3.5 to over 5.0. Correspondingly, training Top-1 accuracy improves from around 45\% to
85\%, whereas validation Top-1 accuracy never exceeds 25\% and remains largely stagnant. Validation Top-5 accuracy
plateaus near 60\%, significantly below the training Top-5 accuracy, which reaches 98\%.

This immediate and sustained separation between the learning curves indicates that the ViT-B-16 model rapidly overfits
the source domain and fails to generalize to the target domain from the very beginning. Unlike the ResNet50 analysis
where the divergence was explicitly triggered by switching the validation set at epoch 6, here the severe domain gap is
present throughout the entire training duration. The results unequivocally demonstrate that standard pre-training on
this source data is insufficient for the target domain, confirming an urgent need for explicit domain adaptation
strategies to bridge this gap.
