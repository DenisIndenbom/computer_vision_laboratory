\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This project addresses the problem of domain shift in computer vision, focusing on the transition from high-quality
synthetic image data to real-world user-generated photographs. A unified training pipeline is developed to investigate
domain adaptation strategies that enable effective transfer between source and target domains. Baseline image
classification models (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16) are implemented and evaluated on the VisDA-2017 benchmark.

A comparative analysis examines performance degradation across domains and assesses the effectiveness of different
adaptation approaches. The project deliverables include a reproducible training pipeline, a public repository of
experimental results, and a course work describing the methodology and findings. The outcomes contribute to a deeper
understanding of domain adaptation challenges and provide practical insights for developing robust computer vision
systems.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Problem Relevance}
Computer vision models often fail when deployed in real-world conditions due to the domain gap between controlled
training data (synthetic/studio) and unpredictable user-generated content. This project systematically analyses the
synthetic-to-real shift, focusing on robustness against common image quality degradations.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
The research investigates the robustness of modern computer vision models under synthetic-to-real domain shift,
focusing on how real-world image variability affect classification performance. The goal is to quantify performance
degradation across domains and explore reliable ways to measure cross-domain generalization.

\subsection{Project Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Develop a structured experimental pipeline for measuring and visualising domain shift from high-quality
	      synthetic/studio images to real-world photographs, using the VisDA-2017 benchmark as a representative testbed.
	\item Train baseline classification models (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16) on the source domain and quantitatively assess the
	      cross-domain performance gap without any adaptation, applying standard data augmentation throughout.
	\item Implement and compare three domain adaptation strategies: discrepancy-based alignment (MMD and CORAL) and adversarial
	      feature learning (DANN), integrating them into both convolutional and transformer backbones.
	\item Carry out a comprehensive comparative analysis across all models and conditions (source-only, adapted, and augmented
	      inputs), examining accuracy degradation, feature distribution shifts via t-SNE/PCA, and relative robustness of CNN and
	      ViT architectures.
	\item Publish a fully reproducible GitHub repository containing the entire training/evaluation pipeline, pretrained
	      checkpoints, and a detailed final report that documents the methodology, experimental findings, and practical
	      recommendations for synthetic-to-real transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

Domain shift between training and deployment distributions remains a fundamental obstacle for visual recognition
systems. Early domain adaptation research introduced statistical alignment methods to reduce this mismatch.
Discrepancy‑based approaches minimise measures such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)~\cite{gretton2012kernel,
	long2015learning} or align second‑order statistics via CORAL~\cite{sun2016deep}. Adversarial strategies, pioneered by
Domain‑Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN)~\cite{ganin2016domain} and later extended by
ADDA~\cite{tzeng2017adversarial}, employ gradient reversal to learn domain‑invariant features. These methods
convincingly show that feature‑space alignment can substantially reduce domain discrepancy without requiring target
labels.

The synthetic‑to‑real gap represents a particularly severe instance of domain shift. High‑quality rendered or simulated
images differ markedly from real‑world photographs in texture, lighting, and background clutter. Large‑scale synthetic
data can be generated from game engines~\cite{richter2016playing}, and adversarial refinement can improve
photorealism~\cite{shrivastava2017learning}. Nevertheless, the domain shift caused by real‑world artefacts -- variable
illumination, blur, sensor noise -- remains challenging. Standard robustness benchmarks such as
ImageNet‑C~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} systematically evaluate model sensitivity to common corruptions, yet they
do not explicitly model the structured distribution mismatch between synthetic and real imagery. The VisDA‑2017
dataset~\cite{visda2017} specifically targets this gap with a large‑scale 12‑class benchmark for synthetic‑to‑real
adaptation.

Vision Transformers (ViTs)~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image} have recently emerged as strong alternatives to convolutional
networks. While their performance on clean data often surpasses that of CNNs, their behaviour under domain shift and
visual corruptions is not yet fully understood, motivating a systematic comparison of transformer and convolutional
backbones.

In summary, a rich body of domain adaptation techniques exists, yet a controlled experimental comparison that couples
modern architectures (ResNet‑50 and ViT‑B/16), representative alignment strategies (MMD, CORAL, DANN), and a
challenging synthetic‑to‑real benchmark under realistic quality variation is still missing. This project addresses that
gap by providing a unified pipeline and an empirical evaluation of these components.

\section{Methods Research \& Dataset Search}
\subsection{Methods of Domain Adaptation}
Several domain adaptation approaches have been proposed in computer vision, with Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, CORAL \cite{sun2016deep} and DANN \cite{long2015learning} among the most widely used in
classification tasks. This work focuses on the study and application of these strategies.

\subsubsection{MMD}
Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is a non-parametric distance metric used to measure the distribution difference between
source and target domains in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The main idea is to minimize the discrepancy
between feature representations of two domains during training.

Given source domain samples $X_s=\{x_i^s\}_{i=1}^{n_s}$ and target domain samples $X_t=\{x_j^t\}_{j=1}^{n_t}$, the
squared MMD distance is defined as

\[
	\text{MMD}^2(X_s,X_t)=\left\|\frac{1}{n_s}\sum_{i=1}^{n_s}\phi(x_i^s)-\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{j=1}^{n_t}\phi(x_j^t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2
\]

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is a feature mapping function into RKHS $\mathcal{H}$. In practice, kernel functions such as
Gaussian RBF kernel are used to compute MMD efficiently.

\subsubsection{CORAL}
Correlation Alignment (CORAL) is a domain adaptation method that aligns the second-order statistics of source and
target feature distributions by minimizing the difference between their covariance matrices.

Let $C_s$ and $C_t$ denote the covariance matrices of source and target features respectively. The CORAL loss is
defined as

\[
	\mathcal{L}_{CORAL}=\frac{1}{4d^2}\|C_s-C_t\|_F^2
\]

where $d$ is the feature dimension and $\|\cdot\|_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm. By matching covariance statistics,
CORAL reduces domain shift while keeping feature representations discriminative.

\subsubsection{DANN}
Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) is an adversarial learning framework that aims to learn domain-invariant
feature representations using a gradient reversal layer.

The objective function consists of two parts: classification loss and domain discrimination loss:

\[
	\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_y(\hat{y},y)-\lambda \mathcal{L}_d(\hat{d},d)
\]

where $\mathcal{L}_y$ is the task-specific loss (e.g., cross-entropy loss), $\mathcal{L}_d$ is the domain
classification loss, and $\lambda$ is a trade-off hyperparameter. The gradient reversal layer multiplies the gradient
by $-1$ during backpropagation to enforce domain confusion and promote domain-invariant features.

\subsubsection{Summary}
In summary, MMD, CORAL, and DANN address domain shift from complementary perspectives: distribution matching in RKHS,
second-order statistical alignment, and adversarial domain confusion. Together, they provide representative strategies
for learning domain-invariant features, forming a solid foundation for the comparative analysis conducted in this work.

\subsection{Suitable Dataset}
VisDA-2017 \cite{visda2017} is the largest synth-to-real dataset, introduced in Visual Domain Adaptation Challenge. It
contains 12 classes, with \textit{$\sim 152K$} training images, \textit{$\sim 72K$} validation images and \textit{$\sim
		55K$} test images. The dataset provides a challenging large-scale setting for unsupervised domain adaptation, making it
highly suitable for research.

\subsection{Key Challenges}
The primary challenge in synthetic-to-real domain adaptation is the significant domain gap: models trained on clean
data struggle with the variability of real-world images, including differences in lighting, texture, and background.

\section{Model and Training Pipeline Implementation}
\subsection{Framework \& Computing power}
As the main machine learning framework, PyTorch was chosen due to its flexibility, performance and the built-in
implementation of ResNet and ViT-B architectures within Torch Vision submodule.

All computations were performed on an RTX 4090.

\subsection{Training Pipeline}
Prior to train models, was implemented fundamental utilities for metrics, loss functions, data loaders and training
procedure. All models were fitted using the pipeline shown in Figure~\ref{fig:training_baseline}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{training_baseline.png}
	\caption{Baseline training pipeline.}
	\label{fig:training_baseline}
\end{figure}

After initialization, the pipeline configures the device and seed, then determines whether to resume training by
loading a checkpoint and restoring the optimizer and RNG state. Once inside the main epoch loop, it executes a training
step comprising batch loading, forward/backward passes, loss computation, weight updates, and metric aggregation. This
is followed by a validation step that performs a forward pass to compute validation loss and aggregate metrics. Each
epoch concludes with metric logging, and an optional checkpoint save, before checking if further epochs are required;
if not, training completes.

\subsection{ResNet50 Training on source domain}
In the first stage to determine baseline score, a ResNet50 model was trained for image classification on the source
(synthetic) domain. The network was initialized with ImageNet weights and optimized using the AdamW optimizer with
learning rate \(1 \times 10^{-5}\) to minimize cross-entropy loss. Standard data augmentation techniques (random
cropping and horizontal flipping) were applied during training.

Figure~\ref{fig:resnet50_baseline} presents the training and validation loss and Top-1/Top-5 accuracy.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{resnet50_baseline.png}
	\caption{\textit{ResNet50 training curves on synthetic data.}}
	\label{fig:resnet50_baseline}
\end{figure}

Training loss drops from 0.57 to 0.007 in 15 epochs, with Top-1 accuracy reaching 99.8\% and Top-5 100\%, indicating
near-perfect source-domain memorization. Validation metrics diverge sharply: loss rises from 2.30 to 3.08, Top-1
accuracy stagnates at 43–44\%, and Top-5 hovers near 75\%. This severe overfitting shows that ResNet50 features do not
generalize to the real domain without adaptation.

\subsection{ViT-B/16 Training on source domain}
Similar to the ResNet50, a ViT-B/16 model was initialized with ImageNet weights and trained with the same
hyperparameters and techniques.

Figure~\ref{fig:vitb16_baseline} presents the training and validation loss and Top-1/Top-5 accuracy.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{vitb16_baseline.png}
	\caption{\textit{ViT-B/16 training curves on synthetic data.}}
	\label{fig:vitb16_baseline}
\end{figure}

Training converges even faster (loss 0.0055, Top-1 >99.8\%, Top-5 100\%). Validation loss drops to 1.42 at epoch 4 then
rises to 1.78 with oscillations. Validation Top-1 accuracy ranges 55.8–61.5\% (mean ~58.5\%), Top-5 85–89.6\%. These
values are substantially higher than ResNet50's, indicating better cross-domain generalization--likely due to ViT's
global attention, which is less biased toward synthetic-specific local textures. Overfitting remains, but the baseline
is clearly stronger.

\subsection{Comparing baselines}
Both models overfit (near-perfect training, low validation accuracy). However, ViT-B/16 outperforms ResNet50
consistently:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Top-1 validation:} ViT ~58.5\% vs. ResNet50 ~43.5\% (+15 p.p.).
	\item \textbf{Top-5 validation:} ViT ~87.5\% vs. ResNet50 ~74.5\%.
	\item \textbf{Validation loss:} ViT 1.5–1.8 vs. ResNet50 >2.8 (more confident predictions on real data).
	\item \textbf{Stability:} ViT's higher level is maintained despite fluctuations; ResNet50's flat accuracy suggests a lower cross-domain ceiling.
\end{itemize}
Thus, pretrained ViT-B/16 provides a superior baseline for synthetic-to-real transfer, making it more promising for
subsequent domain adaptation methods.

\section{Applying Domain Transition methods}
In the following experiments, models are initialized with pretrained ImageNet weights. The weights of the lower layers
are frozen throughout the entire training process, while the higher layers remain unfrozen. The classification heads
are initialized with random weights drawn from a uniform distribution $\mathcal{U}(-\sqrt{k}, \sqrt{k})$, where $k =
	\frac{1}{\text{in\_features}}$. Additionally, the learning rate is set to $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for the classification
head and $1 \times 10^{-5}$ for the unfrozen layers. Thus, these common steps are designed for fast convergence and
stable training.

The models are optimized using the AdamW optimizer to minimize the sum of cross-entropy loss and domain transition
regularization.

The VisDA-2017 dataset was initially separated in three subsets: a training set, a validation set, and a test set,
where the test set remains unlabeled. During the experiments, each training batch consists an equal number of samples
from both the training and test sets. The training samples were used to compute the Cross-Entropy loss, while the test
samples were used for the MMD criterion. Each validation batch comprises samples from the validation set.

while the third and fourth blocks (\texttt{Layer3} and \texttt{Layer4} in Figure~\ref{fig:resnet50_mmd_arch}) remain
trainable. Feature representations for MMD alignment are extracted at two stages: after \texttt{Layer3}
(\texttt{feat\_l3}) and before \texttt{AvgPool} (\texttt{feat\_fl}). The two MMD values are averaged and scaled by
\(\lambda\). The MMD scaling factor \(\lambda\) is set to \(1\).

\begin{figure}[H]

persistent overfitting. The CORAL metric decreases steadily from 0.00218 to 0.00135, showing second-order alignment.
Validation Top-5 accuracy reaches \(\approx\)87.4\%. The \(\sim\)6-point gain confirms some benefit, but it is markedly
smaller than MMD's \(\sim\)12-point gain, reinforcing that higher-order moment alignment is more effective for this
synthetic-to-real task and that second-order covariance alignment alone is insufficient.

\subsection{ResNet-50 with Domain Adversarial Training (DANN)}
In the case of domain adversarial training a gradient reversal layer and domain classification head were consistently
added. The new head consists of two fully linear connected layers, with ReLU and Dropout (\textit{\(p = 0.5\)})
activation functions in between. This architectural solution was chosen in order to create a strong competition between
the feature extractor and the domain classification part of the network. Also, the new head is initialized with random
weights, and reversed gradient is scaled by \(\lambda = 0.7\).

\end{figure}

The results of experiment is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:resnet50_dann}.

	\caption{ResNet50 training curves with with DANN.}
	\label{fig:resnet50_dann}
\end{figure}

ResNet-50 with DANN achieves a final validation Top-1 accuracy of 62.67\% (peak 62.67\% at epoch 20), a substantial
improvement over the baseline (43.5\%). Training loss decreases from 1.58 to 0.69, and training Top-1 accuracy reaches
99.24\%, confirming strong source-domain memorization. Validation loss, however, rises from 1.68 to 2.38, indicating
persistent overfitting. The domain classification accuracy (green curve) declines steadily from 82.4\% to 60.5\%,
demonstrating that the feature extractor learns increasingly domain-invariant representations as the adversarial
training progresses. Validation Top-5 accuracy stabilizes around 90\%, further supporting improved cross-domain
generalization.

The \(\sim\)19-percentage-point gain over the baseline surpasses both MMD (\(\sim\)58.3\%) and CORAL (\(\sim\)44.4\%),
establishing DANN as the most effective domain adaptation method for ResNet-50 on this synthetic-to-real task. The
strong improvement confirms that adversarial feature alignment, which directly encourages domain confusion, is superior
to moment-based alignment (MMD) and second-order covariance alignment (CORAL) for this challenging domain shift.

For ViT-B/16, the gradient reversal layer and domain classification head were added similarly. The domain head consists

by \(\lambda = 0.7\), and the head is initialized with random weights.
